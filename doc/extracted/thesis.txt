[Heading 1] Abstract
[Normal] Security Operations Centers (SOCs) increasingly struggle to keep pace with growing alert volume, analyst shortages, and adversaries that adapt faster than static detection rules. Industry data shows that a typical SOC handles more than ten thousand alerts per day, of which roughly seventy percent are false positives, while the mean time to detect a breach in some organizations still exceeds two hundred days. This combination of noise and latency is the central problem this thesis addresses.
[Normal] This project presents AgeixAISOC, an AI-orchestrated Security Operations Center platform designed around one governing principle: artificial intelligence detects, correlates, scores, and recommends, while a human analyst retains final authority over any consequential action. The platform is built around a LangGraph-based master orchestrator that coordinates a set of specialized agents responsible for threat detection, risk scoring, threat hunting, recommendation generation, and — strictly within an authorized lab scope — red-team validation. These agents operate on top of a live Wazuh SIEM integration used for telemetry ingestion and Sigma rule deployment.
[Normal] A Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) approval workflow governs every high-impact response action before it reaches execution, and every decision, whether generated by an agent or made by a human analyst, is captured in an auditable trail. This design choice directly targets the accountability and trust gaps identified in fully autonomous security-response systems.
[Normal] The system was developed and validated inside an isolated lab network comprising a FortiGate firewall, a Wazuh SIEM manager and agent pair, an Active Directory domain controller, and both vulnerable and attacker hosts. This lab-first approach allowed the detection and orchestration logic to be exercised against realistic, repeatable attack scenarios without any risk to production infrastructure.
[Normal] This document describes the problem motivating the work, the target and as-built architecture, the methodology used to design and validate the platform, the results achieved to date — reported honestly at the subsystem level — and the roadmap of future work, including SOAR integration completion, an expanded threatintelligence fusion layer, and a self-improving detection-gap loop, required to mature AgeixAISOC from a working prototype into a governance-ready platform.

[Heading 1] Introduction
[Heading 2] 1.1 Background and Context
[Normal] Modern organizations generate an overwhelming volume of security telemetry from firewalls, endpoints, identity systems, cloud workloads, and network sensors. A typical Security Operations Center (SOC) analyst is expected to triage this telemetry, correlate it against threat intelligence, judge its severity, and decide on a response — often within minutes, and often for dozens of alerts within a single shift. In practice, this responsibility routinely exceeds what a human team can sustain, and this leads to well-documented failure modes: alert fatigue, missed or delayed detections, inconsistent triage quality between analysts of differing experience levels, and detection rules that fall out of date as adversary techniques evolve faster than signatures can be written and reviewed.
[Normal] According to industry reports, the average SOC handles over ten thousand alerts per day, of which roughly seventy percent are false positives. This volume of noise causes analyst burnout, high turnover, and — critically — leads to missed real threats buried among routine, low-value alerts. The Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) in some organizations reaches as high as two hundred and seven days, a window that gives attackers ample opportunity to move laterally, escalate privileges, and exfiltrate sensitive data long before a human analyst becomes aware that anything is wrong.
[Normal] This gap between the volume of telemetry that must be reviewed and the capacity of human analysts to review it meaningfully is the starting point for this thesis. Rather than treating the volume problem as something to be solved purely by hiring more staff — an approach that does not scale given the well-documented global shortage of cybersecurity professionals — this project explores how a carefully governed set of AI agents can absorb the triage and correlation burden while a human analyst remains the final authority on any action with real-world consequences.
[Heading 3] 1.1.1 The SOC Crisis
[Normal] The cybersecurity industry faces a critical and persistent shortage of skilled professionals. With more than 3.5 million unfilled cybersecurity positions estimated globally, organizations cannot simply hire their way out of the alert-fatigue problem. This shortage, combined with the increasing sophistication and speed of modern attacks, creates an urgent need for intelligent automation that augments — rather than replaces — human analysts.
[Normal] The shortage is not evenly distributed: it is most acute at the tier-1 analyst level, precisely the role most exposed to alert-fatigue and burnout. Analysts in this role are commonly expected to make an initial threat-vs-noise judgment on every incoming alert, a task that is repetitive, high-volume, and cognitively demanding in a way that is well suited to augmentation by a reasoning system, provided that system's output remains explainable and reviewable.
[Heading 3] 1.1.2 The Rise of AI in Security
[Normal] Artificial Intelligence has made significant inroads into cybersecurity, with applications ranging from malware detection and user-behavior analytics to automated phishing classification. However, many AI security solutions operate as "black boxes" — they detect a threat but cannot explain why, which erodes analyst trust and makes the tool difficult to audit or improve. Furthermore, fully autonomous AI systems that take action without human approval pose legal, operational, and reputational risks. A single false positive resulting in a critical production server being isolated can cause a business-disruption incident that costs far more than the security incident it was meant to prevent.
[Normal] This tension — between the clear need for automation at scale, and the equally clear risk of ungoverned automation — is the central design problem that AgeixAISOC was built to address.
[Heading 2] 1.2 The Need for a Human-Governed AI Platform
[Normal] AgeixAISOC is a graduation project that explores how a coordinated set of AI agents, operating under an explicit Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) governance model, can absorb much of the triage and correlation burden while leaving consequential decisions — blocking an IP address, isolating a host, disabling a compromised account — with a human analyst. The project treats the AI as a force multiplier for the SOC analyst, not a replacement for their judgment, and this framing is reflected in every architectural decision described in Chapter 3.
[Heading 3] 1.2.1 Key Requirements for a Modern SOC Platform
[Normal] Explainability — every AI recommendation must include clear, humanreadable reasoning that references the underlying evidence.
[Normal] Human Governance — no high-impact action executes without explicit human approval, recorded against a named analyst.
[Normal] Auditability — every decision, automated or human, must be logged in a form suitable for compliance review and post-incident analysis.
[Normal] Adaptability — the system must be able to learn from analyst feedback rather than requiring constant manual rule maintenance.
[Normal] Local Deployment — data sovereignty and confidentiality requirements favor on-premise, self-hosted operation over sending sensitive telemetry to thirdparty cloud APIs.
[Normal] Modularity — the platform must integrate cleanly with existing security tools (SIEM, firewall, EDR) rather than requiring a wholesale replacement of the SOC stack.
[Heading 2] 1.3 Project Vision and Scope
[Normal] The vision of AgeixAISOC is to create a self-improving security platform in which AI agents handle the heavy lifting of detection, correlation, and recommendation; human analysts make the final decision on any high-impact action; a RetrievalAugmented Generation (RAG) memory layer enables continuous learning from analyst feedback; and a SOAR integration automates response execution once — and only once — that response has been approved.
[Normal] The scope of this graduation project, as delivered, includes:
[Normal] Building a live, isolated VMware-based lab environment representative of a small enterprise network.
[Normal] Integrating Wazuh SIEM as the primary telemetry source and detection-rule deployment target.
[Normal] Developing a LangGraph-based orchestrator that coordinates a set of specialized AI agents.
[Normal] Implementing a Human-in-the-Loop approval workflow with Approve / Modify / Reject / Request-Info outcomes.
[Normal] Creating an auditable trail covering every automated and human decision.
[Normal] Building a RAG-backed memory layer over ChromaDB that reduces repeat false positives over time.
[Normal] Items explicitly outside the delivered scope of this graduation project — but retained as prioritized future work in Chapter 7 — include full SOAR execution against production-grade firewalls beyond the lab FortiGate instance, a broad multi-source threat-intelligence fusion layer, and network detection and response (NDR) via Zeek or Arkime.
[Heading 2] 1.4 Contributions of this Thesis
[Normal] This thesis makes the following contributions:
[Normal] A layered reference architecture for an AI-orchestrated SOC that cleanly separates ingestion, detection, orchestration, human decision, and execution concerns, so that any one layer can be extended or replaced without redesigning the others.
[Normal] A working implementation of that architecture, built on open-source tooling (Wazuh, LangGraph, CrewAI, Ollama, ChromaDB, n8n, FastAPI, React), validated against a live SIEM inside an isolated lab network.
[Normal] A concrete, auditable Human-in-the-Loop workflow, with four defined outcomes (Approve, Modify, Reject, Request-Info) and an accompanying audit-trail schema.
[Normal] A Retrieval-Augmented-Generation feedback loop that converts analyst overrides into a persistent, queryable memory of false positives and confirmed incidents, directly reducing repeat alert fatigue.
[Normal] An honest, subsystem-level account of current implementation status (Chapter
[Normal] 6), distinguishing confirmed-working functionality from partially
[Normal] implemented and target-design components — a level of transparency that is often missing from vendor and academic descriptions of "AI SOC" platforms.

[Heading 2] 1.6 The SOC Tiering Model and Where AgeixAISOC Fits
[Normal] Most conventional SOCs organize analysts into tiers. Tier-1 analysts perform initial triage: they review incoming alerts, discard obvious false positives, and escalate anything that looks genuinely suspicious. Tier-2 analysts investigate escalated incidents in depth, correlating multiple data sources and determining root cause. Tier-3 analysts and threat hunters handle the most complex incidents and proactively search for threats that automated detection has not yet surfaced. This tiering model exists precisely because triage, investigation, and hunting require different depths of context and different time budgets per alert.
[Normal] AgeixAISOC is positioned primarily as a Tier-1 augmentation layer: its Detection, Risk Scoring, and Threat Hunter agents (Section 3.2.1) perform the same category of work a Tier-1 analyst performs — reading an alert, classifying it, and deciding whether it warrants further attention — but at machine speed and across every incoming alert rather than a manually prioritized subset. The HITL gate then hands the resulting, pre-analyzed decision package to a human analyst for the judgment call that closes out the alert. This positioning is deliberate: it targets the specific bottleneck identified in Section 2.1.1 without attempting to replace the deeper investigative judgment that Tier-2 and Tier-3 analysts provide.
[Heading 2] 1.7 Typical Attack Lifecycle and Platform Touchpoints
[Normal] To further motivate the architecture in Chapter 3, it is useful to walk through where AgeixAISOC intervenes in a typical attack lifecycle. An attacker first performs reconnaissance against the target network, for example through port scanning. If the attempt escalates to active exploitation — a brute-force credential attack or exploitation of a known vulnerability — that activity generates traffic and log events at the network and host layers. A properly tuned FortiGate IPS and Wazuh SIEM deployment observes this traffic and raises a correlated alert. From that point, the alert enters the AgeixAISOC pipeline described in Section 5.3: it is analyzed by the agent layer, scored, and presented to a human analyst as a decision package. Only once the analyst approves a response does the Execution Layer act, ideally before the attacker completes lateral movement or achieves their objective.
[Normal] This lifecycle view illustrates why the two confirmed-working capabilities reported in Chapter 6 — SIEM ingestion and the HITL-gated analysis pipeline — represent the highest-value portion of the platform to get right first: they cover the window between initial detection and human decision, which is exactly the window in which analyst time is currently the scarcest resource, as established in Section 1.1.1.

[Heading 1] Problem Definition, Motivation and Objectives
[Heading 2] 2.1 Problem Definition
[Normal] Conventional SOC tooling is largely reactive and rule-based. SIEM platforms centralize log collection but leave correlation and triage almost entirely to human analysts; SOAR platforms can automate response, but only for scenarios an engineer has already anticipated and explicitly scripted. Neither layer reasons about a novel alert the way an experienced analyst does — by weighing context, asset criticality, and known adversary behavior together, in real time, drawing on experience that is difficult to encode as a static rule. This produces three concrete gaps that AgeixAISOC directly targets.
[Heading 3] 2.1.1 Gap 1: Triage Bottleneck
[Normal] Analysts spend the majority of their working time separating benign noise from genuine threats rather than investigating confirmed incidents. With ten thousand or more alerts per day and a seventy-percent false-positive rate, analysts are overwhelmed before they even begin meaningful investigative work. This leads to critical alerts being buried in noise and missed outright, inconsistent triage quality between analysts depending on experience and fatigue level, and, ultimately, burnout and high turnover across SOC teams — which in turn worsens the shortage described in Chapter 1.
[Heading 3] 2.1.2 Gap 2: Static Detection
[Normal] Signature- and rule-based detections do not adapt automatically when a red-team exercise or a real incident reveals a coverage gap. The process of writing, testing, and deploying a new detection rule is manual, slow, and requires specialized expertise in the target SIEM's rule syntax. This means new attack techniques can go undetected for extended periods, organizations remain exposed to previously observed but unaddressed technique variants, and detection coverage silently erodes over time as the environment and the threat landscape both change.
[Heading 3] 2.1.3 Gap 3: Fragmented Accountability
[Normal] When automation is introduced into a SOC without a coherent governance model, it is often difficult to reconstruct why a given action was taken, by which component, and under whose approval. This fragmentation creates compliance and audit challenges during regulatory review, an inability to learn systematically from past decisions, and — perhaps most damaging in practice — reduced analyst trust in the automation itself, which leads analysts to bypass or ignore the very tools meant to help them.
[Heading 2] 2.2 Literature and Industry Review
[Normal] The idea of applying machine learning to security alert triage is not new; anomalydetection and user-behavior-analytics (UEBA) systems have been part of commercial SIEM offerings for over a decade. What has changed more recently is the availability of large language models capable of reading unstructured alert context, correlating it against known frameworks such as MITRE ATT&CK, and producing a human-readable rationale — a capability that earlier rule-based and purely statistical systems lacked.
[Normal] Prior academic work on Explainable AI (XAI) has established that a system's usefulness to a human operator depends heavily on the operator's ability to understand and verify its reasoning, not merely on the system's raw accuracy (Gunning et al., 2019). This finding directly informed the decision to make every AgeixAISOC agent output an explicit natural-language rationale rather than a bare classification label.
[Normal] Separately, research on automated detection-rule generation has shown that mapping observed behavior to MITRE ATT&CK techniques can meaningfully accelerate the process of turning a novel detection into a deployable Sigma rule (Lee et al., 2023), while studies on false-positive rates in SIEM environments confirm that the seventypercent figure commonly cited in industry reports is broadly consistent with SIEM deployments observed in academic evaluations (Hassan et al., 2024). Finally, work specifically addressing human-in-the-loop design for security automation has argued that analyst trust — and therefore real-world adoption — depends on a system that can be overridden, not merely one that is statistically accurate (Ahmed et al., 2022). AgeixAISOC's HITL approval gate, described in Chapter 3, is a direct architectural response to this finding.
[Normal] On the commercial side, established SOAR vendors provide robust playbook execution but generally assume the analyst has already made the classification and prioritization decision; and standalone "AI SOC" startups frequently market autonomous response capabilities without a clearly documented human-override mechanism. AgeixAISOC's position in this landscape is deliberately conservative: it automates the analytical burden (detection, correlation, scoring, recommendation) while keeping the SOAR-style execution step gated behind an explicit, logged human decision.
[Heading 2] 2.3 Motivation
[Normal] The motivation for AgeixAISOC is to demonstrate, in a controlled lab environment, that an orchestrated set of specialized AI agents can perform the detection-torecommendation portion of the SOC workflow reliably enough to be genuinely useful, while a strict human-approval gate prevents the well-known risks of fully autonomous security response — namely, false positives triggering businessimpacting actions, or an attacker manipulating the automation pipeline itself to cause a denial-of-service against the organization's own infrastructure.
[Heading 3] 2.3.1 Academic Motivation
[Normal] The project serves as a practical, hands-on integration of the skills developed throughout the Digilians Cybersecurity track: SIEM operation using Wazuh, network security device configuration on FortiGate, Active Directory administration, offensive-security fundamentals used to generate realistic lab traffic, secure software architecture, and the integration of AI and machine-learning components into a live security workflow. Building a working end-to-end system — rather than a purely theoretical design — was treated as a core requirement of the project from the outset.
[Heading 3] 2.3.2 Industry Motivation
[Normal] The cybersecurity industry is at a crossroads. The shortage of skilled analysts, combined with the increasing volume and sophistication of attacks, demands genuinely useful automation rather than incremental tooling improvements. AgeixAISOC explores a path in which AI handles the volume and combinatorial complexity of triage while humans retain control and accountability — a model that is more defensible, both operationally and from a governance perspective, than either fully manual SOC operation or fully autonomous response.
[Heading 2] 2.4 Objectives
[Normal] The objectives of this project are:
[Normal] Design a layered SOC architecture that clearly separates ingestion, detection, orchestration, human decision, and execution concerns.
[Normal] Implement a central orchestrator that coordinates specialized AI agents for detection, risk scoring, threat hunting, and recommendation generation.
[Normal] Integrate a live SIEM (Wazuh) as the primary telemetry and detection-rule deployment source.
[Normal] Implement a Human-in-the-Loop approval workflow so that no high-impact action executes without explicit analyst sign-off.
[Normal] Build an isolated, realistic lab network in which detection and orchestration logic can be exercised safely against representative attack scenarios.
[Normal] Produce an auditable record of every automated decision and every human decision made against it.
[Normal] Implement a Retrieval-Augmented-Generation memory layer that reduces repeat false positives based on analyst feedback.
[Normal] Identify, honestly, which components are fully working, which are partially working, and which remain future work, so that the platform's maturity is represented accurately in this document and in any subsequent demonstration or defense.

[Heading 2] 3.1 Layered Overview
[Normal] AgeixAISOC is organized into layers that mirror the natural lifecycle of a security alert: from raw telemetry, through AI-driven analysis, to a human decision, to an executed and audited response. This layering is the single most important architectural decision in the project, because it is what allows the human-approval gate in Section 3.3 to sit as a clean, mandatory checkpoint between analysis and execution, rather than being scattered throughout the codebase as ad-hoc checks.

📷 Figure 3.1 — AgeixAISOC Layered Architecture Overview  / Insert the layered-architecture diagram (Ingestion → Normalization → AI  / Orchestration → Agent Layer → HITL → Execution → Audit) exported from the design tool or drawn in the presentation deck.

[Heading 3] 3.1.1 Ingestion Layer
[Normal] The Ingestion Layer receives telemetry from Wazuh and, in the target design, additional sources such as EDR, network sensors, firewall logs, Active Directory event logs, and external threat-intelligence feeds. In the delivered system, this layer handles log collection from the Wazuh manager via a custom webhook integration, normalization of the resulting JSON payload, and initial filtering to remove duplicate or low-severity noise before an alert is passed further down the pipeline.
[Heading 3] 3.1.2 Normalization Layer
[Normal] The Normalization Layer maps heterogeneous log formats into a single common alert schema. This ensures that all downstream components — the agents, the orchestrator, and the dashboard — work with a consistent data model regardless of the originating source, which is what allows new telemetry sources to be added in future work (Chapter 7) without redesigning the agent layer.
[Heading 3] 3.1.3 AI Orchestration Layer
[Normal] The AI Orchestration Layer is a LangGraph-based master orchestrator that sequences agent calls, manages shared state across the pipeline, and enforces the mandatory approval checkpoint before any consequential action. This is the coordinating "brain" of the platform, and its internal structure is described in detail in Section 3.2 and again, at the implementation level, in Chapter 5.
[Heading 3] 3.1.4 Agent Layer
[Normal] The Agent Layer consists of a set of specialized CrewAI agents, each responsible for one well-defined analytical task: detection, scoring, hunting, or recommendation. Each agent has a specific role, a defined input and output schema, and operates independently of the others, which keeps individual agents testable in isolation — a property that proved important during development, since it allowed each agent to be validated against sample alerts before the full orchestrator was wired together.
[Heading 3] 3.1.5 Human-in-the-Loop Layer
[Normal] The Human-in-the-Loop Layer presents AI output to an analyst for approval, modification, or rejection before any response executes. This layer includes the decision-card presentation logic, the Approve / Modify / Reject / Request-Info controls, analyst note capture, and a queryable decision history. It is described in full in Section 3.3.
[Heading 3] 3.1.6 Execution Layer
[Normal] The Execution Layer carries out approved actions against integrated security controls. At the time of writing, SIEM rule deployment (pushing an approved Sigma rule into Wazuh) is fully implemented and confirmed working; SOAR-driven firewall and EDR actions form part of the target design and are currently blocked by an authentication issue described in Chapter 6.
[Heading 3] 3.1.7 Audit / Governance Layer
[Normal] The Audit / Governance Layer records every agent output, every human decision, and every execution result for traceability. This ensures compliance with regulatory record-keeping requirements, supports reconstruction of incident investigations after the fact, and enables continuous improvement of the agent prompts and orchestration logic through systematic review of past decisions.

[Heading 2] 3.2 Orchestrator and Agent Layer
[Normal] At the core of the platform is a LangGraph Master Orchestrator that coordinates a set of specialized CrewAI agents. [Heading 3] 3.2.2 Agent Workflow
[Normal] The orchestrator follows a fixed high-level sequence for every incoming alert:
[Normal] Alert Reception — the Wazuh alert is normalized and passed to the orchestrator as the initial pipeline state.
[Normal] Parallel Analysis — the Detection, Risk Scoring, and Threat Hunter agents run concurrently against the same normalized alert.
[Normal] Synthesis — the Recommendation Agent synthesizes the three parallel outputs into a single proposed action, with an accompanying rationale.
[Normal] HITL Checkpoint — the proposed action, together with all supporting agent output, is presented to a human analyst as a decision package.
[Normal] Execution — only upon explicit approval is the action executed via the appropriate adapter (SIEM rule deployment or, in the target design, a SOAR action).
[Heading 3] 3.2.3 Orchestrator State Management
[Normal] LangGraph manages state through a shared state object that persists across every node in the graph. This object contains the current alert information, the output of every agent that has run so far, the human decision once made, the execution status of any approved action, and the accumulated audit-trail entries for that alert. Passing state explicitly through the graph, rather than relying on side effects or a shared mutable database record, keeps the pipeline's behavior predictable and makes it straightforward to replay a given alert through the pipeline for debugging.

[Heading 2] 3.3 Human-in-the-Loop Governance
[Normal] A guiding design principle of AgeixAISOC is that the AI orchestrates — it detects, correlates, scores, and recommends — but the human decides. Any action with realworld impact, such as blocking an IP address, isolating a host, disabling a user account, or deploying a new detection rule, is routed through an approval workflow with four possible outcomes: Approve, Modify, Reject, or Request More Information. Every decision, along with the analyst's identity, a timestamp, the original AI recommendation, and the final action actually taken, is written to the audit trail described in Section 3.1.7.
[Heading 3] 3.3.1 HITL Workflow
[Normal] The AI generates a decision package containing a threat description, the mapped MITRE technique, a numeric risk score, and a recommended action.
[Normal] The decision package is presented to the analyst through the dashboard's Pending HITL panel.
[Normal] The analyst reviews the underlying evidence and the AI's stated reasoning before making a decision.
[Normal] The analyst selects one of the four available actions: Approve, Modify, Reject, or Request Info.
[Normal] If Approve or Modify is selected, the (possibly adjusted) action is executed by the Execution Layer.
[Normal] If Reject or Request Info is selected, the decision is logged, the alert is either closed or escalated, and — critically — the outcome is written back into the RAG memory layer described in Section 5.5 so that similar future alerts benefit from this analyst judgment.

[Heading 2] 3.6 Scalability Considerations
[Normal] Although the delivered lab environment comprises six hosts, the layered architecture in Section 3.1 was deliberately designed with larger deployments in mind. The Ingestion and Normalization layers are stateless with respect to any single alert, which means additional Wazuh managers or additional telemetry sources can be added by extending the ingestion adapters without modifying the orchestrator or agent layer. Because the orchestrator's parallel-analysis step (Section 3.2.2) is itself the natural unit of horizontal scaling, running multiple orchestrator instances behind a queue is the intended path to handling higher alert volumes, though this has not yet been implemented or load-tested and is noted as a gap alongside the future-work items in Chapter 7.
[Heading 2] 3.7 Data Handling and Privacy Considerations
[Normal] Because AgeixAISOC processes security telemetry that may include internal IP addressing, usernames, and process command lines, two design choices were made specifically to limit unnecessary data exposure. First, LLM inference is performed locally via Ollama rather than being sent to a third-party cloud API, so raw telemetry never leaves the isolated lab network during agent reasoning. Second, the RAG memory layer (Section 5.5) stores embeddings and derived fields such as MITRE technique IDs and IP subnets rather than full raw payloads wherever the embedded field list in Section 5.5.1 allows, reducing the amount of sensitive raw data retained in the long-lived vector store.
[Heading 2] 3.8 Design Rationale: Why LangGraph and CrewAI
[Normal] Two frameworks were evaluated for the orchestration and agent layers before LangGraph and CrewAI were selected. A simpler, hand-rolled sequential-functioncall pipeline was considered first, but was rejected because it could not cleanly express the parallel-analysis step described in Section 3.2.2, nor could it naturally represent a pause-and-resume checkpoint for the HITL gate without significant custom state-machine code. LangGraph's explicit state graph provided both of these properties out of the box: nodes can run in parallel where the graph topology allows, and a node can be defined to block until an external event — the analyst's decision — is received.
[Normal] CrewAI was chosen for the agent layer specifically because it treats each agent as an independently configurable unit with its own role, goal, and backstory prompt, and its own tool set, which matched the modularity requirement in Section 1.2.1 more directly than writing each agent as a bespoke LangChain chain would have. This separation is what allowed, for example, the Detection Engineering Agent (Section 3.2.1) to be added to the roster of agents after the core four-agent pipeline was already stable, without touching the Detection, Risk Scoring, or Threat Hunter agents' code.

[Heading 1] Methodology
[Heading 2] 4.1 Development Approach
[Normal] The platform was developed iteratively rather than as a single monolithic build. Each subsystem — SIEM integration, the orchestrator, individual agents, the HITL workflow, and the audit trail — was built and tested against the lab environment before being connected to the next subsystem in the chain. Priority was deliberately given to getting the detection-to-recommendation pipeline working end-to-end against real Wazuh alerts before extending breadth, for example by adding additional data sources or additional specialized agents beyond the core four.
[Normal] This iterative, integration-first approach was chosen specifically to avoid a common failure mode in multi-component AI projects, in which every individual component appears to work in isolation but the full pipeline fails the first time it is connected end-to-end. By validating the narrowest possible working pipeline first — Wazuh alert in, human decision package out — the team ensured that every subsequent addition (a new agent, a new data source, a new dashboard view) was extending a system that was already known to work, rather than being the first true integration test.
[Heading 2] 4.2 Lab Environment
[Normal] All detection logic, agent behavior, and orchestration flows were developed and validated inside an isolated lab network rather than against production infrastructure. This allowed representative attacks to be safely generated and observed end-to-end, without any risk of the training or testing process itself causing disruption to a live environment.

[Heading 2] 4.3 Testing Approach
[Normal] Agent and orchestrator behavior was validated by generating alerts in Wazuh from actions performed on the lab hosts — for example, brute-force login attempts, exploitation of Metasploitable 2, and Sysmon-visible process activity on the Windows 10 endpoint — and confirming that the pipeline correctly ingested the alert, produced a coherent agent analysis, and surfaced the result for human approval within the dashboard. Sigma rule deployment was validated separately by confirming that an approved rule appeared and took effect inside the Wazuh manager, and by generating fresh matching traffic to confirm the newly deployed rule actually fired.
[Normal] Testing was organized around three questions asked of every scenario: did the alert reach the orchestrator at all; did the resulting decision package contain a reasonable, defensible recommendation given the underlying evidence; and did the HITL gate correctly block execution until an analyst decision was recorded. A scenario was only marked as validated once all three questions could be answered affirmatively and reproducibly.

[Heading 2] 4.5 Ethical and Operational Considerations
[Normal] All offensive activity performed as part of this project — including the Nmap scans, brute-force attempts, and exploitation of Metasploitable 2 used to generate test telemetry — was conducted exclusively inside the isolated, host-only lab network described in Section 4.2 and Appendix A, with no route to production infrastructure or the public internet beyond the controlled NAT path used for software updates. The Red Team Validation Agent described in Section 3.2.1 is explicitly scoped to this lab environment only, and its use outside an authorized lab context is out of scope for this project and is not supported by the current implementation.
[Heading 2] 4.6 Risk Analysis During Development
[Normal] Three risks were identified and actively managed during development. First, the risk that LLM-generated recommendations would be unpredictable or inconsistent across runs was mitigated by constraining every agent's output to a defined structured schema and by preferring smaller, more deterministic models (Section 5.2.1) for the highest-frequency classification tasks on the critical path. Second, the risk that lab-generated attack traffic could inadvertently affect systems outside the isolated network was mitigated by the strict host-only network segmentation described in Section 4.2 and Appendix A, with no route to production infrastructure. Third, the risk that the HITL gate could be silently bypassed by a bug in the orchestrator was mitigated by treating the Execution Node (Section 5.1.1) as unreachable from any code path other than the HITL Checkpoint Node, and by verifying this directly during the testing described in Section 4.3.

[Heading 2] 5.1 Orchestration Layer
[Normal] The LangGraph master orchestrator implements the alert-to-decision pipeline as a directed graph of agent calls, with explicit state passed between nodes and a designated node representing the human-approval checkpoint. This graph-based structure allows the flow to be extended with additional agents or additional approval gates without restructuring the entire pipeline — a property that was exercised in practice when the Detection Engineering Agent was added after the core detectionto-recommendation flow was already stable.

[Normal] Threat Hunting Node — invokes the Threat Hunter Agent.
[Normal] Synthesis Node — combines the outputs of the three analysis nodes into a single candidate recommendation.
[Normal] HITL Checkpoint Node — pauses the graph and waits for a recorded human decision before proceeding.
[Normal] Execution Node — executes the approved action through the appropriate adapter and records the result.

[Heading 3] 5.1.2 State Management
[Normal] The orchestrator maintains state as a single dictionary-like object containing the original alert data, the detection result, the risk score, the hunting result, the synthesized recommendation, the human decision once recorded, and the execution result. Passing this object explicitly between nodes, rather than relying on a shared external store, keeps each node's behavior a pure function of its input state, which materially simplified debugging during development.
[Heading 2] 5.2 Agent Layer
[Normal] Each of the specialized CrewAI agents is implemented as an independently callable unit with a defined input schema — the normalized alert plus any relevant enrichment context — and a defined output schema, such as a MITRE technique mapping, a numeric risk score, or a recommended action with rationale. This modularity keeps individual agents testable in isolation from the rest of the orchestrator, which was essential given that the agents were developed and refined over several iterations as prompt quality and tool integration improved.

[Heading 2] 5.5 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Architecture
[Normal] The AgeixAISOC platform uses a Retrieval-Augmented Generation subsystem to provide historical context to the AI agents. Rather than relying solely on the static pre-trained weights of the underlying language models, the RAG infrastructure continuously captures security telemetry, incident resolutions, and analyst overrides, converting them into vector embeddings stored in ChromaDB. When a new alert enters the pipeline, the RAG engine performs a real-time semantic search to retrieve the top-K most similar historical security events, which reduces false positives by allowing agents to recognize recurring benign patterns and previously flagged falsealarm triggers.
[Heading 3] 5.5.1 Embedding Model and Chunking Strategy
[Normal] Chunking Strategy — fixed-size JSON key-value extraction with a slidingwindow overlap.
[Normal] Chunk Size — 512 tokens per embedded document block, with a 64-token overlap between adjacent chunks.
[Normal] Embedded Fields — rule_description, mitre_technique_id, source_ip_subnet, payload_samples, and resolution_notes.
[Normal] Embedding Model — a local sentence-transformer (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), chosen specifically for isolated lab deployment without external API dependencies.
[Normal] Distance Metric — cosine similarity over normalized vector representations.
[Heading 3] 5.5.2 Vector Database Collections
[Normal] ChromaDB maintains two distinct collections to keep live incident memory separate from confirmed benign baselines:
[TABLE START]
Collection | Purpose
ageix_incident_memory | Validated threats and executed responses (was_false_positive: false).
ageix_benign_baselines | Analyst-rejected false positives (was_false_positive:  / true).

[Heading 2] 5.6 RAG Memory Lifecycle and Feedback Loop
[Normal] To ensure vector memory remains accurate and current, the platform implements a continuous update cycle driven directly by analyst decisions. Every validated incident execution generates a new document entry in ageix_incident_memory with was_false_positive set to false; if a security analyst overrides an action through the HITL dashboard by selecting Reject, the event is written, or updated, in ChromaDB with was_false_positive set to true instead.
[Normal] A suppression guard then uses this memory: when a future alert matches a vector tagged was_false_positive: true at a similarity of 0.90 or higher, the system automatically lowers that alert's priority score, or suppresses it outright, to prevent repeated false alarms of the same kind. This adaptive-learning loop is the primary mechanism by which AgeixAISOC reduces alert fatigue over time without requiring constant manual rule maintenance — directly addressing the Triage Bottleneck gap identified in Section 2.1.1.

[Heading 2] 6.4 Discussion
[Normal] The results confirm the platform's central hypothesis on a limited but real scale: a coordinated agent pipeline can process a live SIEM alert through detection, scoring, and recommendation, and a human-approval gate can be reliably enforced before any consequential action executes. [Heading 3] 6.4.1 Key Learnings
[Normal] LangGraph is well suited to SOC workflows — the state-graph approach worked well for orchestrating a sequence of agent calls with a mandatory human checkpoint.
[Normal] CrewAI agents provide good modularity — each agent could be developed, prompted, and tested independently before full pipeline integration.
[Normal] HITL is critical for trust — analysts in informal testing were markedly more willing to accept AI recommendations when given a genuine, logged ability to override them.
[Normal] Real-world integration is the hardest part — SIEM and firewall integrations required by far the most careful, iterative debugging of any part of the project, more than the AI reasoning components themselves.

[Heading 2] 6.6 Case Study Walkthrough: SSH Brute Force Incident
[Normal] To make the abstract data flow of Section 5.3 concrete, this section walks through one representative, fully-observed incident from first packet to closed audit record. The scenario was generated deliberately from the Kali attacker host against the Windows 10 target described in Table 4.1, and is one of the scenarios summarized in Table 6.2.
[Heading 3] 6.6.1 Step 1 — Attack Generation
[Normal] From the Kali attacker host, a scripted SSH credential-stuffing attempt was launched against the monitored Windows 10 endpoint, generating forty-seven failed authentication attempts within a three-minute window. This traffic pattern was chosen specifically because it is a well-understood, low-ambiguity trigger for a brute-force detection rule, making it a suitable first end-to-end validation scenario.
[Heading 3] 6.6.2 Step 2 — Detection and Ingestion
[Normal] The Wazuh agent installed on the Windows 10 host, together with Sysmon, forwarded the corresponding authentication-failure events to the Wazuh manager. A built-in Wazuh correlation rule fired once the failure count crossed its threshold within the configured time window, generating a single high-severity alert. This alert was forwarded via the custom webhook integration described in Section 3.4 to the AgeixAISOC ingestion endpoint within seconds of the rule firing.
[Heading 3] 6.6.3 Step 3 — Agent Analysis
[Normal] The normalized alert entered the orchestrator's Ingestion Node and proceeded through the Detection, Risk Scoring, and Threat Hunter nodes in parallel, as described in Section 5.1.1. The Detection Agent classified the pattern as consistent with MITRE technique T1110 (Brute Force) under the Credential Access tactic. The Risk Scoring Agent combined this classification with the target host's asset criticality and the time of day to produce a numeric risk score in the high range. The Threat Hunter Agent queried recent telemetry for related activity from the same source and found none beyond the triggering alert itself, which was itself informative — it indicated an isolated attempt rather than a broader, multi-host campaign.
[Heading 3] 6.6.4 Step 4 — Recommendation and RAG Lookup
[Normal] The Synthesis Node combined these three outputs and queried the RAG memory layer described in Section 5.5 for similar historical events. Because no closely matching entry existed in the ageix_benign_baselines collection at the required similarity threshold, the alert was not suppressed, and the Recommendation Agent proposed a perimeter IP block against the source address as the primary action, with a documented rationale referencing the technique mapping and risk score.
[Heading 3] 6.6.5 Step 5 — Human Decision
[Normal] The resulting decision package was surfaced on the dashboard's Pending HITL panel. The reviewing analyst confirmed the source IP was external and unrelated to any known maintenance or monitoring activity, and selected Approve. The decision, the analyst's identity, and a timestamp were written to the audit trail before execution proceeded, consistent with the HITL workflow described in Section 3.3.1.
[Heading 3] 6.6.6 Step 6 — Execution and Audit
[Normal] Because this scenario targeted the SIEM-side response rather than the firewall — to avoid the FortiGate authentication issue documented in Section 6.2.1 — the approved action in this particular trace resulted in enhanced monitoring and Sigmarule confirmation rather than a firewall block. The full JSON trace for this class of scenario, including the fields recorded at each pipeline stage, is shown in Figure 5.3 (Section 5.3.1). The completed record, including the original alert, every agent output, the analyst's decision, and the execution result, remains queryable from the audit trail for later review.
[Heading 3] 6.6.7 Outcome
[Normal] This walkthrough validates, on a single concrete incident, the same claim made in aggregate in Section 6.4: the pipeline correctly moved a real SIEM alert through detection, scoring, hunting, and recommendation, produced an explainable decision package, and could not proceed to execution without an explicit, audited human decision.

[Heading 1] Conclusion
[Normal] AgeixAISOC demonstrates that an AI-orchestrated detection and recommendation pipeline, built around a live SIEM and governed by an explicit Human-in-the-Loop approval workflow, is achievable within the scope of a graduation project, and provides a credible foundation for a production-oriented SOC platform rather than a purely theoretical proposal.
[Heading 2] 8.1 Summary of Achievements
[Normal] Architecture Design — a layered architecture was designed that cleanly separates ingestion, detection, orchestration, human decision, and execution concerns.
[Normal] Orchestrator Implementation — a LangGraph-based master orchestrator was successfully implemented, coordinating specialized CrewAI agents through a defined state graph.
[Normal] SIEM Integration — live Wazuh SIEM integration was achieved for both alert ingestion and Sigma rule deployment, and confirmed working end-to-end.
[Normal] HITL Workflow — a Human-in-the-Loop approval workflow was implemented, ensuring no high-impact action executes without explicit analyst approval.
[Normal] RAG Memory — a Retrieval-Augmented-Generation feedback loop was implemented, allowing analyst overrides to reduce repeat false positives over time.
[Normal] Lab Environment — an isolated lab network was built and validated against realistic, repeatable attack scenarios.
[Normal] Audit Trail — every automated and human decision is captured for full traceability.
[Heading 2] 8.2 Key Contributions
[Normal] A practical integration of AI agents into a real SOC workflow, demonstrating how AI can augment — not replace — human analysts.
[Normal] HITL as a concrete governance mechanism, providing a reusable template for responsible AI deployment in security contexts.
[Normal] A modular, extensible architecture that can be extended with additional agents, data sources, and response actions without a redesign.
[Normal] A build based entirely on open-source tooling — Wazuh, CrewAI, LangGraph, Ollama, and ChromaDB — supporting on-premise, datasovereign deployment.
[Heading 2] 8.3 Lessons Learned
[Normal] AI is a force multiplier, not a replacement — the HITL approach was well received in informal testing precisely because it preserved analyst authority.
[Normal] Real-world integration is genuinely challenging — SIEM and firewall integrations required more detailed, iterative debugging than the AI-reasoning components.
[Normal] Mock data is a necessary stepping stone — while not ideal for a final deliverable, mock data enabled frontend development to proceed in parallel while backend features were still being completed.
[Normal] Iterative development works — building and testing each subsystem independently, before wiring it into the full pipeline, avoided the kind of integration failures that are otherwise common in multi-agent AI systems.
[Heading 2] 8.4 Final Statement
[Normal] AgeixAISOC represents a meaningful step toward the future of SOC operations — one in which AI handles the volume and combinatorial complexity of modern security telemetry, while humans retain control, accountability, and the final decision on any action with real consequences. The platform is not yet complete: the FortiGate SOAR integration remains blocked by an authentication issue, and several dashboard views are still populated with representative rather than live data. But the core pipeline is working end-to-end against a live SIEM, the architecture is sound and extensible, and the roadmap to a fully governance-ready platform, laid out in Chapter 7, is clear and concretely scoped.
[Normal] This project demonstrates that, with careful layered design, open-source tooling, and an unwavering focus on human governance, building an AI-orchestrated SOC platform is not only theoretically possible but practically achievable within the timeframe of a diploma graduation project — and that reporting its current limitations honestly is itself part of building a platform that a real SOC could eventually trust.

[Heading 1] Appendix J 	Sample SOAR Playbook: Brute Force Response
[Normal] The following is the investigation checklist and response-option menu presented to the analyst as part of the Brute Force Playbook referenced in Section 3.2.1 (Recommendation Agent) and Section 5.7 (Execution Layer). It illustrates the level of explanation provided alongside every proposed action, consistent with the Explainability requirement in Section 1.2.1.
[Heading 2] Investigation Checklist (performed before any action is taken)
[Normal] Verify the source IP is external and not an internal scheduled backup or monitoring tool.
[Normal] Check for concurrent alerts originating from the same source address.
[Normal] Confirm whether the targeted account exists and is currently active.
[Normal] Review NetFlow data for any data transfer following the failed attempts.
[Heading 2] Available Responses
[Normal] Block IP — recommended when the source is confirmed external; creates a firewall address object and blocks all further traffic from that source. Risk: may block a legitimate user behind a shared IP (VPN/NAT).
[Normal] Redirect to Honeypot — recommended for intelligence gathering; redirects the source to an isolated deception segment where subsequent activity is fully logged without alerting the attacker.
[Normal] Isolate Target Host — used when compromise is suspected; moves the target to a quarantine VLAN, preserving forensic evidence at the cost of service disruption.
[Normal] Monitor Only — used when the analyst is not yet confident in the classification; enables enhanced logging without taking any disruptive action.
